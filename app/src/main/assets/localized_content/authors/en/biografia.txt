Neville Lancelot Goddard (1905–1972) was a prophet, an influential teacher, and an author. As a metaphysician, he did not associate himself with any “ism” or school of “New Thought,” as these collective groups commonly claim. Goddard was sent to illuminate the teachings of psychological truth contained in the Bible and to restore awareness of the meaning the ancients intended to convey to the world.

Neville Goddard was born on February 19, 1905, in St. Michael, Barbados, in the British West Indies. He was the son of Joseph Nathaniel Goddard, a merchant, and Wilhelmina née Hinkinson. Neville was the fourth child in a family of nine boys and one girl. In 1922, at the age of seventeen, he traveled to the United States aboard the S.S. Vasari to study drama. He became a dancer and, during this period, married his first wife. They had a son together, Joseph Neville Goddard. While touring England with his dance company, he became interested in metaphysics after a conversation with a Scotsman who lent him a series of books on the powers of the mind. When he returned to New York, he left the entertainment industry to devote his full attention to spiritual and mystical study. Neville Goddard’s first marriage was brief, and he remained single for years. In the 1930s he met his second wife, a designer. After they married, they had a daughter named Victoria, or “Vicky.” In 1943, at the age of thirty-eight, he was drafted into the U.S. Army. He did not want to serve, particularly because he believed he was too old to become a soldier and had a wife and daughter at home to care for. Through the power of imagination, as Neville recounted in his March 24, 1972 lecture, he received an honorable discharge after only a few weeks of training. One consequence of his brief military service was that he obtained full U.S. citizenship, having remained a British citizen until then.

Abdullah—Neville Goddard’s teacher

Goddard’s interest in esoteric interpretations of the Bible deepened after he met Abdullah, an Ethiopian Jewish teacher who lectured on esoteric Christianity and taught both Goddard and Joseph Murphy. Neville went to hear him somewhat reluctantly, yielding to a friend’s persistent urging. He later said: “I remember the first night I met Abdullah. I had deliberately put off attending one of his meetings because a man whose judgment I did not trust had insisted that I go. At the end of the meeting, Ab came over to me and said, ‘Neville, you are six months late.’ Surprised, I asked how he knew my name. He replied, ‘The brothers told me you would be here six months ago.’ Then he added, ‘I will remain until you have received everything I must give you. Then I will leave.’ He may well have wished to leave, but he had to wait for me.” From this first meeting onward, Neville studied with Abdullah, learning Hebrew, the Kabbalah, and the hidden symbolic meaning of Scripture.

Neville Goddard’s lectures

After traveling extensively throughout the United States, Neville eventually made his home in Los Angeles. During the 1950s, he delivered a series of talks on television and radio, and for many years he regularly lectured to large audiences at the Wilshire Ebell Theatre. In the 1960s and early 1970s, he limited most of his lectures to Los Angeles, New York, and San Francisco. In his early lectures and books, Neville focused exclusively on what he called the Law: the technique of creating one’s physical reality through imagination. This aspect of his work most closely aligns with the teachings of the New Thought movement. In describing the Law, Neville recounted how, during the Great Depression and without money of his own, he made a sea voyage from New York to visit his family in Barbados. He also explained how he used the power of imagination to receive an honorable discharge from military service and continue his lectures during the Second World War. In his San Francisco talks during the 1950s and 1960s, he shared accounts of how other people had used the Law. Speaking on television in the Los Angeles area, he said: “Learn how to use your power of imagination lovingly on behalf of others, for man is moving into a world where everything is subject to his power to imagine.”

The Promise and the Law

In 1959, Neville began to experience what he called “the Promise.” He later wrote: “I knew nothing of the Promise until I began to experience it and it unfolded within me, beginning that summer and continuing for the next three and a half years. And this is biblical; read it in the Book of Daniel, where it is described as ‘a time, times, and half a time.’ It amounts to 1,260 days in your experience of it.” In the late 1960s and early 1970s, Neville placed greater emphasis on the Promise than on the Law. A person could use the power of imagination to change circumstances, he said, but the result would be temporary “…and it will vanish like smoke.” He went on to explain that the Promise superseded the Law: “Oh, you can use it [the Law] to make a fortune, to become known in the world—all these things can be done—but your true purpose here is to fulfill Scripture.” After subordinating the Law to the Promise, he became deeply enthusiastic about hearing and sharing the accounts of those who had experienced the Promise, just as he had previously done with the Law. Neville’s theology of the Promise includes both union with Divinity after death and a future restoration for those who do not receive the Promise during their lifetime. Of the Promise, he said: “You do not earn it; it is a gift, entirely grace. God’s Promise is unconditional; God’s Law is conditional, and it comes in its appointed time. If you do not experience it in this life, you pass through a door—that is all death is—and you are instantly restored to life in a world like this one, simply another such world.” There, he said, you continue with the same problems you had here, without losing your identity—not old, blind, or disabled if you left this life that way, but young. Of the people in this restoration after death, he said: “They grow, marry, and die there too, with all the fear of death that we have here. And if they die there without experiencing the Promise, they are restored to life again and again in the place best suited to the work that still must be completed in them. This continues until ‘Christ is formed in you,’ and as ‘sons of the resurrection’ you leave this world of death, never to enter it again.” When asked about the fear of eternal hell and damnation, Neville answered with Scripture: “None shall be lost in all my holy mountain. You are God, and how could God eternally condemn himself?” Until we awaken and make this discovery, he said, we have the privilege of using a Law given by God to “cushion the blows of life.” In Neville’s words, the Law can be stated briefly: “Imagining creates reality.” In the final years of his life, he said: “I know my time is short. I have completed the work for which I was sent, and now I am eager to depart. I know I will not appear again in this three-dimensional world because the Promise has been fulfilled in me. As for where I am going, I will know you there as I have known you here, for we are all brothers, infinitely in love with one another.” Neville Goddard died in Los Angeles on October 1, 1972, at the age of sixty-seven.
