Below are practical exercises, supported by neuroscientific evidence, to develop and improve focused attention. They are clearly explained, include why they work in the brain and concrete examples of everyday application.

🔸 Focused attention is trained, it is not a fixed trait. These exercises work because they strengthen the neural circuits responsible for executive control and reduce cognitive interference. The key is not the duration, but the regularity and quality of the focus.


🟢 1. Attention to breathing (focused meditation)

What does it consist of?

Sit quietly and direct your attention exclusively to your breathing (sensation of air entering and leaving through the nose or the movement of the abdomen). When the mind wanders, the distraction is recognized and one returns, without judgment, to the respiratory focus.

Neuroscientific evidence

This exercise has been shown to increase activation and cortical thickness in the prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex, regions involved in attentional control and self-regulation. Additionally, it reduces default network reactivity associated with mind wandering.

How to practice it
    • Duration: 5–10 minutes daily (increase progressively).
    • Comfortable posture, straight back.
    • Single focus: breathing.
    • Key rule: notice the distraction and come back.

Practical example

Before studying or working on a complex task, performing 5 minutes of focused breathing improves the quality of sustained attention during the subsequent session.



🟢 2. Deep reading with sustained attention

What does it consist of?

Read a text without interruptions, without music, notifications or multitasking, keeping your attention on the content and deep understanding, not on speed.

Neuroscientific evidence

Attentive reading activates fronto-temporal circuits and strengthens working memory. When “quick scanning” is avoided, consolidation in the hippocampus and conceptual integration are favored.

How to practice it
    • Time: 20–30 minutes.
    • Moderately challenging text.
    • Underline or take notes only at the end of a paragraph.
    • If your mind wanders, consciously reread the last sentence.

Practical example

Read a scientific article or a technical chapter and, when finished, explain the content in your own words without looking at the text.



🟢 3. Deliberate single-tasking

What does it consist of?

Perform a single cognitive task for a defined period, eliminating any other parallel activity (mobile phone, email, unnecessary tabs).

Neuroscientific evidence

Multitasking reduces the efficiency of the prefrontal cortex and increases the “attentional switching cost.” Single-task training improves the stability of executive networks.

How to practice it
    • 25–45 minute blocks.
    • Define a clear and measurable task.
    • Eliminate external stimuli before starting.
    • Brief pause between blocks.

Practical example

Program for 30 minutes without switching windows or checking messages, focusing only on solving a specific problem in the code.



🟢 4. Conscious sensory observation exercise

What does it consist of?

Choose a sensory stimulus (an object, sound or flavor) and observe it with total attention, exploring its details without automatically mentally labeling them.

Neuroscientific evidence

This exercise improves sensory discrimination and selective attention, strengthening connections between sensory and prefrontal areas.

How to practice it
    • Duration: 3–5 minutes.
    • Observe texture, shape, temperature or sound.
    • Avoid judgments (“I like it”, “I don't like it”).
    • Return to the stimulus if thoughts appear.

Practical example

Hold a cup of coffee and pay attention to its weight, temperature and aroma before drinking, without distractions.



🟢 5. Attentional counting with zero error

What does it consist of?

Mentally count repetitions (breaths, steps, sounds) while maintaining attention. If you lose count, you start over.

Neuroscientific evidence

This exercise trains sustained attention and early detection of distraction, skills linked to the anterior cingulate.

How to practice it
    • Count breaths to 10.
    • When you reach 10, return to 1.
    • If there is distraction, restart.

Practical example

During a calm walk, count your steps up to 20 without losing focus.



🟢 6. Deliberate practice with immediate feedback

What does it consist of?

Train a specific skill with maximum attention, clear objectives and constant error correction.

Neuroscientific evidence

Focused attention during deliberate practice increases dopamine release, facilitating synaptic plasticity in specific circuits.

How to practice it
    • Choose a specific microskill.
    • Practice in short and intense intervals.
    • Check errors immediately.

Practical example

Learn a mathematical or technical concept by solving problems one by one, consciously analyzing each failure.



🟢 7. Attentional diet (reduction of stimuli)

What does it consist of?

Consciously limit constant exposure to highly distracting stimuli (notifications, social networks, digital noise).

Neuroscientific evidence

Overstimulation reduces the capacity for sustained attention by hyperactivating rapid reward dopaminergic systems.

How to practice it
    • Disable non-essential notifications.
    • Establish fixed schedules for networks.
    • Create low-stimulus environments.

Practical example

Work on your phone outside the room during deep study sessions.
