Epigenetics: when the mind dialogues with biology

From neuroscience, epigenetics is understood as the set of mechanisms that regulate which genes are activated or silenced without changing the DNA. The brain, through thoughts, emotions and perceptions, influences systems such as the endocrine and immune systems, releasing neurotransmitters and hormones that act as biological “switches.” In other words, we are not prisoners of our genes: the internal environment (what we think and feel) and the external environment (habits, stress, relationships) modulate genetic expression. Thus, subjective experience leaves measurable traces on the body.

Under this scientific perspective there is a meeting point with the shared thought of Neville Goddard, Joe Dispenza, Gregg Braden and Bruce Lipton: the idea that consciousness—thoughts, emotions and beliefs—actively participates in biology.

Goddard, from a philosophical and symbolic language, maintained that imagination creates reality. Although he did not talk about genes or synapses, his proposal fits with modern epigenetics: sustaining a repeated mental state (for example, trust or fear) involves activating specific neural circuits that, over time, translate into stable hormonal patterns. These patterns are, precisely, epigenetic signals. Imagine someone who visualizes themselves calmly and confidently every day before speaking in public: their nervous system learns that response and reduces stress activation.

Joe Dispenza translates this intuition into the language of neuroscience: recurring thoughts create recurring chemical states. If a person lives trapped in worry, their brain reinforces anxiety networks and their body chronically secretes cortisol, affecting the expression of genes associated with inflammation or immunity. Changing your thinking—through mindfulness, visualization, or meditation—involves teaching the brain a new state, and the body a new chemistry. Epigenetics explains how this new chemistry can “reprogram” genetic expression.

Gregg Braden emphasizes the role of the heart and emotional coherence. For him, emotions such as gratitude or compassion generate ordered signals that harmonize the body's systems. From epigenetics, this is understood as a favorable internal environment: when the autonomic nervous system comes into balance, cells receive consistent messages that facilitate repair and adaptation processes. A simple example: practicing daily gratitude can improve heart variability, an indicator linked to better physiological responses to stress.

Cell biologist Bruce Lipton puts it directly: beliefs act as a filter that translates the environment into biology. His studies on cell membranes showed that it is not the nucleus (where the DNA is) that “rules”, but rather the signals that the cell receives. In practical terms, a constant threat belief turns the world into a hostile environment for the cell; a security belief does the opposite. Epigenetics is the bridge that explains this effect.

Together, these authors agree on a key idea: it is not about magical thinking, but about understanding that the mind is part of the environment that regulates our genes. Changing mental and emotional habits does not replace biology; the guide. Thus, epigenetics invites us to assume creative responsibility: each thought held is a signal, and each signal, a possibility of transformation.
