Epigenetics, mind and anticipation of the future in the teachings of Joe Dispenza

Joe Dispenza popularizes a particular interpretation of epigenetics by integrating it with neuroscience, physics, and meditation. In its central approach, genes do not act as a fixed destiny, but as potentialities that are expressed or silenced depending on internal and external signals. Among these signals, Dispenza gives a priority role to thoughts, emotions and mental states sustained over time.

From its approach, epigenetics demonstrates that the environment influences genetic expression, but Dispenza goes a step further by redefining what it means by “environment.” For him, the environment is not limited to physical factors such as diet, stress or toxins, but includes the internal environment: the emotional chemistry that generates the habitual way of thinking and feeling. Thus, a recurring emotion—such as fear, guilt, or gratitude—would act as a biochemical signal that constantly dialogues with DNA.

One of the key concepts in his teachings is the idea of ​​“signaling the gene ahead of the environment.” This involves training the mind and body to live a desired future experience before it physically occurs, now generating the emotions that would normally arise after the event. According to Dispenza, when a person feels gratitude before receiving what they want, or well-being before there is an external cause, they are sending a coherent biological signal that prepares the organism for a new genetic expression.

A practical example would be a person who wants to improve their health. Instead of waiting until you feel good to be grateful, start each day by evoking the emotion of vitality and balance as if your body is already functioning optimally. In Dispenza's model, this sustained emotion acts as a message that “informs” the genes that the body already lives in a new state, favoring changes at a physiological level. The future, in this sense, becomes the cause, and the present the effect.

Another common example in his teachings is the professional field. A person who sees himself confidently performing a new job, and who feels in advance the satisfaction and security associated with that role, would be conditioning his biology to respond coherently when real opportunities appear. The body stops living in survival mode—ruled by the past—and begins to adapt to a mentally rehearsed scenario.

In summary, Joe Dispenza proposes a vision in which epigenetics not only responds to the external environment, but also to anticipated subjective experience. Thinking and feeling a future experience would not be a simple exercise of imagination, but a biological act with measurable consequences in mind-body coherence. Although its approach combines science with interpretive and philosophical elements, its central message invites us to assume active responsibility for internal states, understood as the first link in the chain of personal change.
