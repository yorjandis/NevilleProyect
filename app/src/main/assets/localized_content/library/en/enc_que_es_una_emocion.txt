What are emotions

Emotions are the result of our brain constantly interpreting what is happening in our body. Every heartbeat, every breath, every muscle tension sends signals that our brain reads and interprets. That interpretation is what we feel as emotion.

Neuroscientifically, emotions are defined as functional states of the brain that involve:
    1. Physiological component: changes in the autonomic nervous system (heart, breathing, sweat).
    2. Behavioral component: facial expressions, posture, reflex movements.
    3. Subjective component: conscious experience (feeling joy, sadness, fear).

1. Primitive component: the ancient brain

At the base of our emotions is what we could call the primitive brain, made up mainly of:
    • Midbrain and brainstem: control automatic responses, such as jumping when faced with a loud noise or tensing muscles when faced with danger.
    • Amygdala: detects threats or important things very quickly, even before we are aware of them.

These structures produce immediate chemical and physical responses:
    • Increased heart rate.
    • Release of adrenaline and other hormones.
    • Muscle tension or sweating.

This is what allows us to survive: our body reacts before we have time to think. For example, if we see a snake, our heart races before we realize the danger.


2. Cognitive component: conscious interpretation

Then the more “modern” part of the brain comes into play, mainly the prefrontal cortex. This area acts as an interpreter of all the signals that our body sends.
    • Observe changes in the heart, breathing, muscles.
    • Combine those cues with the context: where we are, what happened before, what we learned.
    • Gives a conscious meaning to what we feel: fear, joy, surprise, sadness...

Everyday example:
    • Your heart beats fast and your hands sweat.
    • The amygdala detects potential danger (something is moving quickly next to you).
    • The prefrontal cortex analyzes the situation: “Oh, it's a dog running towards me, it's not dangerous.”
    • Result: you feel a slight alarm, but not intense fear.
If the same sign occurred in front of a lion, the interpretation would be: panic.

This shows that emotion is not only in the body or only in the mind, but in the interaction between the two.


3. Summary:

We can think of emotions as a kind of “story that our brain tells itself about our body”:
    1. The primitive brain triggers automatic reactions.
    2. The cognitive brain interprets these reactions according to the context.
    3. The combination of both generates what we feel as emotion.

Simply put: we don't feel fear or joy from the external object itself, but from how our body responds to it and how our mind interprets those responses.
