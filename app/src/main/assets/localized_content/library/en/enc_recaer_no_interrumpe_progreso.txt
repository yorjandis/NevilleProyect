Understanding why relapse does not break the progress of a habit is key because many people give up just when, neurobiologically, they are on the right track.

Below is an explanation of what happens in the brain when there is a relapse and why it does not cancel progress, unless it is misinterpreted.


🟢 1. Common conceptual error: thinking in terms of “all or nothing”

The idea of “I relapsed, so I started again” does not exist in neuroscience.

Because?
Because the brain does not function as a switch, but as a system of cumulative synaptic weights.

📌 A relapse is a specific activation of a circuit, not its complete reinstallation.



🟢 2. What is a relapse on a physiological level (exactly)

When you relapse, this happens:
    • The usual circuit is temporarily reactivated
    • Briefly increases striatal neuronal firing
    • Dopamine is released weaker than before the change
    • The alternative circuit does NOT disappear

👉 You do not return to the initial state.
There is only a temporary rebound.



🟢 3. Neural analogy (very precise)

Imagine two pathways in the brain:
    • Road A (old habit): wide, paved
    • Path B (new habit): narrower, but already marked

Every time you choose B:
    • It widens
    • It is reinforced
    • It becomes cheaper energetically

A relapse is:

Use A once, don't rebuild the highway.

📌 The brain remembers the entire previous history, not just the last event.



🟢 4. The most important thing: relapse occurs with a different brain

This is essential.

Before the change:
    • Striatum dominated
    • Weak prefrontal cortex
    • Strong anticipatory dopamine

After weeks of work:
    • Greater prefrontal control
    • Lower dopaminergic response
    • More awareness of impulse

👉 Even if you do the same behavior, the neural state is not the same.

That's why many people say:

“I relapsed, but it doesn't feel the same anymore.”

That is a sign of progress, not failure.



🟢 5. What a relapse does NOT do

Neurobiologically, a relapse does NOT:
    • Erases the synaptic pruning that has already started
    • Eliminates myelination of the new circuit
    • Suddenly restores anticipatory dopamine
    • Disable pre-learning

All of that would require sustained repetition, not an isolated event.



🟢 6. When a relapse DOES reinforce the old habit

This is important and very specific.

A relapse reinforces the old habit only if:
    1. It is repeated several times in a row
    2. It is accompanied by cognitive self-justification
    3. You live like “it doesn't matter, that's it”
    4. It is associated with emotional relief without an alternative

In that case:
    • LTP (potentiation) is reactivated
    • Dopamine + habit is once again associated
    • The circuit recovers energy advantage

📌 It's not the relapse, it's the interpretation and repetition.



🟢 7. What happens if you manage the relapse well (this is gold)

If this happens after a relapse:
    • You become aware (“this is the old circuit”)
    • You don't punish yourself
    • You resume the alternative habit at the next signal

Then something very interesting happens:

🧠 Corrective memory reconsolidation

That is to say:
    • The brain reactivates the memory of the habit
    • But he keeps it with lower value
    • The signal → reward association is weakened

👉 Relapse becomes a learning tool.



🟢 8. The role of stress (why many relapses don't count)

Most relapses occur when:
    • There is extreme fatigue
    • High cortisol
    • Inhibited prefrontal cortex

In that state:
    • The brain does not learn well
    • Only executes automations

📌 Behavior done under high stress consolidates little.

That's why a relapse on a bad day:
    • Has little synaptic weight
    • Does not redefine the circuit



🟢 9. Simple neurobiological rule (what really matters)

The brain doesn't learn from what you do once,
learn from what you do most of the time
after the appearance of the signal.

That is the real criterion of change.



🟢 10. Final idea (the most important)

A relapse does not break progress
unless it makes you abandon the process.

From the physiological point of view:
    • Progress is cumulative
    • It is not deleted by a specific event
    • Only reversed by sustained repetition
