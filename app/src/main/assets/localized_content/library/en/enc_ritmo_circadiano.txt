Circadian rhythm regulation by sunlight is a profoundly biochemical process that connects the retina, brain, and cellular gene expression. Let's break it down step by step.



🟢 Light as a primary biochemical signal

When sunlight enters the eyes, it is not only useful for seeing. It activates special cells in the retina called intrinsically photosensitive ganglion cells (ipRGCs).

These cells contain a photopigment called melanopsin, which is especially sensitive to blue light (~480 nm), which is very abundant in morning sunlight.

What happens at the molecular level?
    1. Photons activate melanopsin.
    2. A G protein is activated (pathway similar to the visual transduction cascade).
    3. An electrical signal is generated.
    4. That signal travels through the retinohypothalamic tract to a key brain structure.



🟢 The suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN): the master clock

The light signal reaches the suprachiasmatic nucleus, located in the hypothalamus.

This nucleus is the “central clock” of the organism.

What does it do biochemically?

The SCN regulates the expression of clock genes within its neurons:
    • CLOCK
    • BMAL1
    • PER (Period)
    • CRY (Cryptochrome)

These genes work in feedback loops:
    1. CLOCK and BMAL1 activate the transcription of PER and CRY.
    2. PER and CRY proteins accumulate.
    3. When they reach a certain level, they inhibit CLOCK/BMAL1.
    4. They degrade.
    5. The cycle starts again (~24 hours).

This mechanism is purely biochemical and occurs in almost all cells in the body.

Light works by readjusting this molecular loop every day.



🟢 Melatonin suppression

One of the most important effects of sunlight is to inhibit the secretion of melatonin.

The signal from the SCN reaches the pineal gland and inhibits the enzyme:
    • AANAT (arylalkylamine N-acetyltransferase)

This enzyme converts serotonin into melatonin.

At night:
    • Darkness → ↑ AANAT → ↑ melatonin

With sunlight:
    • SCN activation → ↓ AANAT → ↓ melatonin

Melatonin is the biochemical signal of “it's night.”

That's why morning sun exposure:
    • Quickly lowers residual melatonin
    • Marks the beginning of the biological cycle



🟢 Regulation of cortisol and metabolism

Sunlight also influences the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

The SCN modulates the release of:
    • CRH → ACTH → cortisol

Cortisol has a natural peak in the morning (Cortisol Awakening Response).

This peak:
    • Increases blood glucose
    • Activates metabolism
    • Increases alertness
    • Synchronizes peripheral clocks (liver, muscle, adipose tissue)

At a molecular level, cortisol acts on glucocorticoid receptors that also regulate clock genes in peripheral tissues.



🟢 Peripheral clock synchronization

Each organ has its own molecular clock.

Examples:
    • Liver → regulates gluconeogenesis depending on time of day
    • Pancreas → modulates insulin sensitivity
    • Intestine → rate of absorption
    • Muscle → variation in energy efficiency

Sunlight synchronizes the NSQ, and it synchronizes the entire system by:
    • Nervous signals
    • Cortisol
    • Body temperature
    • Eating rhythms

Without adequate light, peripheral clocks become desynchronized.



🟢 Biochemical consequences of not exposing yourself to the sun

When sun exposure is insufficient:
    • The circadian phase is delayed
    • PER/CRY expression is altered
    • Decreases the amplitude of the cortisol peak
    • Insulin sensitivity is altered
    • Increases basal inflammation (metabolic desynchronization)

This explains why artificial light at night can induce:
    • Metabolic alterations
    • Higher cardiovascular risk
    • Sleep problems



🟢 Why is sunlight more powerful than artificial light?

Because:
    • Intensity: 10,000–100,000 lux (outdoor) vs 100–500 lux (indoor)
    • Full spectrum
    • High proportion of natural morning blue
    • Signal consistent with human evolution

Melanopsin needs sufficient intensity to robustly activate the circadian signal.



🟢 Simplified biochemical summary

Sunlight promotes the production of melanopsin which in turn produces SCN that regulates clock genes such as CLOCK/BMAL1/PER/CRY, these genes suppress melatonin and produce a physiological cortisol spike. All this leads to systemic synchronization of the organism. In code it would be:

Sunlight → melanopsin → SCN → CLOCK/BMAL1/PER/CRY gene regulation → melatonin suppression + cortisol peak → systemic metabolic synchronization.
