Time: an experience of mind and consciousness

From neuroscience, time is not an object that the brain “sees”, but rather an internal construction. Our brain does not have a central clock; Instead, different neural networks coordinate memories, expectations, emotions, and bodily states to create the sense of past, present, and future. When we remember, we activate memory circuits; When we anticipate, we use those same circuits to simulate scenarios. Therefore, at the brain level, remembering the past and imagining the future is, to a large extent, the same process. Time, then, is not experienced as an external line, but as a subjective experience shaped by attention and emotion.

From this scientific basis, the thinking shared by Neville Goddard, Joe Dispenza, Gregg Braden and Bruce Lipton converges on a powerful idea: time is neither rigid nor absolute; It is malleable from consciousness.

For Neville Goddard, time is an already complete stage. The past, present, and future exist as simultaneous possibilities, and what we call “future” is simply a state that we have not yet occupied with our attention and feeling. When a person vividly imagines a desired outcome and it feels real, they are not waiting for time to bring it: they are aligning with a version of themselves that already lives it.

Joe Dispenza connects this vision with neuroplasticity. He explains that when we repeat thoughts and emotions from the past, the brain and body become trapped in the same “now” over and over again. We live, biologically, in the past. Instead, when we direct attention to a high future intention and accompany it with coherent emotions, the brain begins to reorganize as if that future were already happening. Thus, the future begins to influence the present, something that challenges the classic idea of ​​linear time.

Gregg Braden provides the bridge between science and ancient wisdom: time, he says, responds to the internal state of the observer. In states of emotional coherence—when mind and heart are aligned—the perception of time changes. Profound moments can feel eternal, and long periods can pass in an instant. This suggests that we do not travel through time; Time responds to our conscience.

Bruce Lipton completes the picture from biology. For him, cells always live in the present, reacting to the environment and the signals they receive. When our mind interprets reality from fear or lack, the body responds as if that threat were happening now. But if we change the perception, we change the signal, and with it the biological response. In this sense, the body does not distinguish between a real event and an intensely imagined one.

Together, these authors propose a transformative vision: time is not just something that passes, but something that participates with us. The past lives in memory, the future in intention, and the present is the point where both meet. Learning to consciously inhabit that present—with aligned thoughts, emotions, and beliefs—is, they say, the key to experiencing more flexible time and a more deliberate life.
