Feeling as a creative force

Various contemporary thinkers have agreed on an idea as simple as it is powerful: we do not create reality only with what we think, but with what we feel. From different approaches—spiritual, neuroscientific and biological—Neville Goddard, Joe Dispenza, Gregg Braden and Bruce Lipton point out that feeling is the true driving force of personal change and life experience.

To understand it simply, let's think about a seed. The thought would be the seed itself: the idea, the intention. But the feeling is the water, the light and the constant care. Without these, the seed does not germinate. In the same way, an idea without emotion remains on the mental plane; It does not have enough strength to transform into experience.

Neville Goddard explained that feeling is internally assuming that something is already real. It is not about wanting out of lack, but about experiencing in advance the emotion of having achieved what you want. For example, it is not enough to think “I want financial peace of mind”; The creative act occurs when a person allows themselves to feel relief, security, and gratitude, as if that stability were already part of their life.

From neuroscience, Joe Dispenza adds a key piece: the body learns through emotion. When a thought is accompanied by an intense feeling, the brain releases chemicals that strengthen new neural connections. In simple words, the body begins to believe before the rational mind, and when the body believes, the person acts in a manner consistent with that new internal state.

Gregg Braden connects this idea with the heart. According to their approaches, the heart not only feels, but amplifies the intention. A thought charged with coherent emotion—such as gratitude or love—has greater impact than a repeated statement without feeling. For example, saying “everything is going to be okay” while feeling genuinely calm is much more effective than repeating the phrase anxiously.

Bruce Lipton, from biology, completes the picture by explaining that the most influential environment of our cells is our internal perception. Constant feelings of fear or stress activate survival responses in the body, while emotions such as trust or security promote growth and well-being. Thus, feeling is not something abstract: it has real physical consequences.

Overall, the message is clear and accessible: feeling is not an emotional luxury, it is a creative act. Consciously choosing how we feel—even for small moments a day—is beginning to shape our experience. It is not about denying reality, but about training the internal world so that the external world has a new direction. When thought and feeling align, creation stops being a desire and becomes a living process.


What is a feeling?
From neuroscience, a feeling is the conscious experience that arises when the brain interprets and makes conscious an emotion generated in the body. The process begins automatically: when faced with a stimulus (a memory, a situation, an idea), the brain activates certain neural circuits—mainly in the limbic system—that trigger physiological responses such as changes in heart rate, breathing, muscle tension or the release of neurotransmitters and hormones. When the brain “reads” these bodily changes and gives them meaning, the feeling appears. For example, increased pulse and muscle activation can be interpreted as excitement or fear, depending on the context and mental interpretation.

The important thing is that the feeling does not occur only in the brain, but in the constant interaction between brain and body. The prefrontal cortex and insula play a key role in integrating bodily signals with thoughts, memories, and beliefs. Therefore, two people can feel different things in the same situation. From this perspective, a feeling is an internal signal that informs the brain about the state of the organism and guides decisions and behaviors. In practical terms, when a feeling is maintained over time, it reinforces certain neural circuits, making that emotional state become more familiar and easier to repeat, directly influencing how we perceive and respond to reality.
