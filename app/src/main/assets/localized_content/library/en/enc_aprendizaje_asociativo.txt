Associative learning, from neuroscience, is the process by which the brain relates two or more stimuli, or a stimulus with an action and its consequence, and modifies its neuronal activity so that this relationship is stored and can influence future behavior.

In other words: the brain learns what things go together and what predicts what.



🟢 Neuroscientific definition

At the brain level, associative learning involves plastic changes in synapses (synaptic plasticity), where the repeated and coordinated activation of neurons strengthens or weakens specific connections.
The key idea is Hebb's principle:

“Neurons that fire together, wire together.”



🟢 Main types of associative learning

🔸1. Classical conditioning (Pavlovian)

You learn to associate two stimuli.

classic example
    • Neutral stimulus: sound of a bell
    • Unconditioned stimulus: food
    • Answer: salivation

After repeated associations:
    • Bell → salivation

What happens in the brain
    • The amygdala and cerebellum play a key role.
    • Connections between sensory areas (hearing) and autonomic circuits (salivation) are reinforced.
    • The neutral stimulus begins to activate the same neural networks as the biologically relevant stimulus.

everyday example
    • Feeling anxious when you hear the sound of a work email.
    • Smell a perfume and immediately remember a person.



🔸2. Operant (instrumental) conditioning

You learn to associate a behavior with a consequence.

Example
    • Action: study
    • Consequence: good grade
    • Result: increased study behavior

Neuroscience behind
    • Key circuit: dopaminergic system (ventral tegmental area → nucleus accumbens).
    • Dopamine acts as a prediction error signal:
    • More reward than expected → increases dopamine.
    • Less reward → decreases.

This adjusts the probability of repeating the behavior.

Practical example
    • Check your cell phone and receive a rewarding message → reinforcement of the habit.
    • Trading: a profitable operation reinforces a strategy (even if it was random).



🟢 Fundamental neural mechanisms

Synaptic plasticity
    • Long-term potentiation (LTP): strengthening of synapses.
    • Long-term depression (LTD): weakening of synapses.

These modifications occur mainly in:
    • Hippocampus (associative and spatial memory)
    • Amygdala (emotional associations)
    • Basal ganglia (habits and reward learning)



🟢 Role of neurotransmitters
    • Glutamate: learning and plasticity (NMDA and AMPA receptors).
    • Dopamine: reinforcement, motivation and reward learning.
    • Norepinephrine: relevance and attention.
    • Acetylcholine: attention-dependent learning.



🟢 Examples applied to daily life

Food
    • Associate ultra-processed foods with immediate pleasure (dopamine).
    • Learning to associate natural food with long-term well-being requires retraining associations.

Emotions
    • A repeated negative experience can associate a place or person with fear.
    • Neurobiological basis of phobias and anxiety disorders.

Learning and study
    • Always studying in the same environment can create contextual associations.
    • Associating learning with positive emotions improves memory consolidation.



🟢 Summary:

From neuroscience, associative learning is:
    • A process of physical change in the brain.
    • Based on synaptic plasticity.
    • Guided by prediction, reward and emotion.
    • Fundamental for survival, habits and decision making
