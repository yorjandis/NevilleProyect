Procedural memory is a type of long-term memory that, from neuroscience, is defined as the system responsible for learning, storing and executing motor and cognitive skills and habits automatically, without the need for conscious attention.

It is the memory of “knowing how,” in contrast to declarative memory, which is “knowing what.”



🟢 Main features
    1. Implicit and unconscious
You don't need to actively think about what you do. Execution happens fluidly and automatically.
    2. It is acquired by repetition and practice
The more you repeat an action, the more robust the neural imprint becomes.
    3. Very resistant to forgetting
Once consolidated, it can be maintained for years, even decades.
    4. Difficult to verbalize
You can do the action perfectly, but it is difficult for you to explain it step by step.



🟢 Practical examples
    • Learn to ride a bicycle
Even if you go years without doing it, your body “remembers” how to maintain balance.
    • Drive a car
At first it requires conscious attention; Over time, many movements become automated.
    • Play a musical instrument
The fingers move alone without thinking about each note.
    • Typing on the keyboard without looking
You don't consciously know where each key is, but your hands do.
    • Cognitive habits
How to solve certain types of mathematical problems or playing patterns in chess.



🟢 Neuroanatomical bases

From neuroscience, procedural memory depends mainly on subcortical circuits, not the hippocampus.

Key structures

1. Basal ganglia
    • Especially the striatum (putamen and caudate nucleus).
    • They are essential for the selection and automation of motor sequences.
    • Closely related to reinforcement learning (dopamine).

👉 Therefore, in diseases like Parkinson's, procedural learning is altered.



2. Cerebellum
    • Adjusts precision, timing and coordination of movement.
    • Allows you to correct errors and refine skills with practice.

Example: progressively getting better at throwing a ball or playing a complex musical passage.



3. Motor and premotor cortex
    • They store already consolidated movement patterns.
    • With practice, cortical activation becomes more efficient and focused.



🟢 Neurotransmitters involved
    • Dopamine
It is key in procedural learning, especially in the reward and error signal.
    • Glutamate
Facilitates synaptic plasticity (LTP and LTD).



🟢 How a procedural memory is formed (step by step)
    1. Cognitive phase
Requires conscious attention (many mistakes).
    2. Associative phase
Errors are reduced, patterns are refined.
    3. Autonomous phase
The execution is fast, stable and automatic.

This model explains why at the beginning you “think too much” and then you just do.



Difference with declarative memory
Procedural memory:
• Implicit
• Automatic
• Basal ganglia and cerebellum
• Skills and habits

Declarative memory:
• Explicit
• Conscious
• Hippocampus
• Facts and episodes

An interesting fact:
Patients with severe amnesia (hippocampal damage) can learn new procedural skills even if they do not remember practicing them.



🟢 Practical applications
    • Neurological rehabilitation
    
Use of repetition and automation to recover motor functions.
    • Education and training
    
Distributed practice outperforms theoretical study for complex skills.
    • Change of habits
    
Habits are better reprogrammed with repetition and reward than with willpower
